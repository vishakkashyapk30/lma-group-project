\begin{abstract}
Legal-aid lawyers in India prepare bail applications from messy case files: scanned and photographed orders, Hindi and English, several documents merged in one PDF. We describe Vakeel-Sahayak.ai, a bail-preparation engine built as an agent harness rather than a single model call. Six agents (intake, dossier and rights checks, retrieval orchestration, drafting, prosecution sparring, batch triage) work over ten typed stores on four shelves. An orchestrator reads a short evidence contract for each of twelve bail factors and decides which stores to query, in which order, and when to stop. Every claim in a draft is checked against its sources, and a draft changes only where a dependency changed. Two approval gates are enforced in code, and client text passes a pseudonymisation boundary before any external model sees it. We evaluate without manual annotation: gold labels come from court text, with a rule labeler and a non-Qwen model labeler that must agree and quote a verbatim span. We compare the full system with seven simpler baselines (single pass, single store, whole-document embeddings, naive chunking, query-every-store, a learned store router, and an expert rule list) and with 18 single-switch variants taken from recent agent and RAG papers. This report describes the system and the evaluation design, and states what has run so far. On the 50-case development set (OpenAI \texttt{gpt-6-luna}, hosted, earlier code) the full system's factor recall is 0.25 on the 8 orders that have a scorable gold factor, below the baselines (0.50 to 0.88) with no gap resolved; it is clearly ahead of the single-pass and single-store baselines on objection recall (0.69 against 0.00, 20 orders) and behind the query-every-store baseline (0.93), with an unsupported-fact rate of 0.87 against 0.76 to 0.98 for the baselines and a cost of about \$0.011 per case. On the current code, with OpenAI on 28 to 38 orders per arm, removing the sparring agent lowers objection recall by 0.75 (95\% interval $-0.94$ to $-0.53$), removing check and repair lowers answerable coverage by 0.42 and citation precision by 0.39, and on OpenAI the query-every-store baseline still matches or exceeds the full system on objection recall. The same two effects appear on a second hosted model (Sarvam-105B, 30 orders: removing the sparring agent lowers objection recall by 0.45, removing check and repair lowers answerable coverage by 0.38), and removing the dossier lowers the unsupported-fact rate on all three model families we ran. Full is clearly ahead of the single-pass baselines on objection recall and, on Sarvam, on citation precision (0.78 against at most 0.02 for the baselines that cite), but it does not beat every baseline: its unsupported-fact rate is not lower than any baseline's on any family, so the claim that it beats all baselines is not supported yet. An architecture search on Sarvam-105B (30 development orders, then a 20-order holdout) traced Full's losses to four causes and fixed them with four opt-in switches; the resulting system, c1457, was registered with five hypotheses before a sealed 80-order blind slice was opened once. On that slice c1457 wins 46, ties 69 and loses 5 of its comparisons with the seven baselines (Full: 29, 76 and 15). Four hypotheses hold: it beats Full on objection recall and answerable coverage, beats the two retrieval baselines of comparable capability on citation precision and fabricated citations, has a lower unsupported-fact rate than every baseline, and needs fewer store calls than the query-every-store baseline. The fifth, no loss outside four cells declared structural in advance, fails on one cell: the learned-router baseline writes fewer unsupported sentences in absolute number, though more as a share. The development numbers are preliminary: small $n$.
\end{abstract}
