\begin{abstract}
Legal-aid lawyers in India prepare bail applications from messy case files, and a model asked to draft one from memory invents facts and case law. We ask whether an agent harness that plans retrieval per bail factor over typed legal stores, checks every claim against its sources and spars with a simulated prosecutor writes better-grounded bail briefs than simpler systems on the same model. Vakeel-Sahayak.ai has six agents over ten typed stores, two counsel approval gates enforced in code and a pseudonymisation boundary before any hosted model. Gold labels come from court text through a rule labeler and a model labeler that must agree on a verbatim span; nothing is annotated by hand. On development orders the first full system did not beat every baseline; an architecture search on Sarvam-105B traced its losses to four causes and fixed them with four opt-in switches. The resulting system, c1457, was registered with five hypotheses before a sealed slice of 80 test orders was opened once. Against seven baselines (single pass, single store, whole-document and chunked retrieval, query-every-store, a learned store router, an expert template) c1457 has 46 wins, 69 ties and 5 losses (the first full system: 29, 76, 15). Four hypotheses hold: it beats the full system on objection recall and answerable coverage, cites real authorities where the comparable retrieval baselines fabricate them (fabricated-citation rate 0.00 against 0.64 and 0.95), states fewer unsupported facts than every baseline, and needs 41\% fewer store calls than querying every store. The fifth fails on one unsupported-sentence count. The verdicts are the same when the outputs are scored against rule-only gold labels. No export happened without counsel's approval.
\end{abstract}
