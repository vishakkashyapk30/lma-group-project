\section{Summary}

Vakeel-Sahayak.ai helps a lawyer prepare a regular bail application in an Indian High Court. Counsel drops in a bail file in whatever state it comes (scanned, photographed, in Hindi, several documents merged into one PDF), or dictates the facts. Six agents, named after the people in an Indian court office, turn it into a court-ready application:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Munshi} (ingestion) reads every page and files what it finds into the right store.
\item \textbf{Peshkar} (dossier) builds a source-checked dossier, a List of Dates and a rights check.
\item \textbf{Sutradhar} (orchestrator) decides, for each bail factor, what to fetch from which of ten knowledge stores.
\item \textbf{Vakeel} (drafter) writes grounds that cite only facts on record and real judgments.
\item \textbf{Abhiyojak} (prosecutor) attacks the draft the way the prosecution would.
\item \textbf{Pairokar} (batch desk) triages hundreds of files at once for a legal-aid office.
\end{itemize}

Counsel approves the dossier and the final draft, and nothing is exported without that approval. The system also deals with the 2024 change from the IPC to the BNS: a case charged under a new BNS section still finds the older IPC case law that applies, and the user is warned when the wording has changed.

The project uses all five course capabilities (fine-tuning, RAG, reasoning, agentic behaviour and tool calling); Section 6 explains where each one is used and how it is measured. All models are open-weight, and no manual annotation is needed: training data and test labels are built automatically from court text, official tables, existing datasets or construction.

\section{Domain and Target User}

\textbf{Domain:} Regular bail in Indian courts, under section 439 of the CrPC (now section 483 of the BNSS). Our data and evaluation use High Court orders, because these are publicly available in bulk.

\textbf{Primary users:} Legal-aid panel lawyers and junior advocates. They draft many bail applications a month, often with little research support.

\textbf{Secondary users:} Law students in supervised legal-aid clinics, and legal-aid offices that must find, among many undertrial prisoners, those who may be entitled to release now.

\textbf{Not users:} The system is not meant for accused persons, the public or judges. It prepares draft material that the user reviews and edits, and it never predicts whether bail will be granted.

\section{Problem Statement}

To write a bail application, a lawyer has to collect facts that are spread across several court documents, such as how long the accused has been in custody, whether the charge sheet has been filed, and whether co-accused have been released. These facts must then be argued against the factors that courts weigh, with case law for each point. The file itself is the first obstacle: it is often one PDF that mixes scanned orders, phone photos of a Hindi FIR and handwritten notes. Since 1 July 2024 the BNS and BNSS have replaced the IPC and the CrPC, so new FIRs cite new section numbers while almost all bail case law still discusses the old ones. General-purpose language models are a poor fit, because they invent facts and case citations: studies of US law found hallucination rates of 58 to 88 percent on case-specific questions [\href{https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.01301}{1}], and 17 to 33 percent even in commercial legal research tools [\href{https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20362}{2}]. Underneath, this is also an engineering problem: the system must ingest messy inputs, keep knowledge of different shapes (statute text, a section-mapping graph, judgment paragraphs, a citation network, what each side argues, the case's own timeline) in separate stores, decide what to fetch from where for each factor, keep the draft consistent as the case moves, and scale to hundreds of files, all on an 8 GB laptop GPU and shared 2080 Ti nodes, with no paid APIs and no manual labels. Existing Indian legal AI mostly predicts outcomes or simulates courtroom debate, and we are not aware of a system that does these things together. A useful tool must therefore tie every fact to its source, cite only real and applicable authority, and say clearly what it could not verify.

\section{Research Questions and Hypotheses}

\subsection{Research Questions}

\textbf{RQ1 (orchestration):} Suppose an orchestrator plans, for each bail factor, which knowledge stores to query, and stops when the evidence is sufficient. Compared with a single LLM pass, single-store RAG, whole-document embeddings, naive chunking, and querying every store and fusing the results, does it produce better-grounded bail briefs, at equal or lower token cost?

\textbf{RQ2 (agentic ingestion):} As case files get messier (scanned, photographed, Hindi, bundled), does page-level agentic routing with gap-driven re-reading keep the dossier more accurate than fixed parsing pipelines? And at what compute cost?

\subsection{Hypotheses}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{H1a:} The full system has higher recall of argued factors (G1) and a lower unsupported-fact rate than each of B0 to B4 (Section 10).
\item \textbf{H1b:} The full system uses fewer tokens and store calls than B4 (every store, no orchestrator), at equal or better quality.
\item \textbf{H1c:} On BNS-era cases, retrieval with section-family expansion, reasoning-only paragraphs and the factor filter has higher recall@10 of court-cited precedents (G2) than plain hybrid retrieval.
\item \textbf{H2:} On degraded, photographed, Hindi and bundled inputs, Munshi's downstream field accuracy (G3) beats the fixed pipelines P1 to P3 and P5, and comes within a margin fixed on the development set of P4 (the vision model on every page) at a fraction of its GPU time.
\item \textbf{F1 to F8:} One hypothesis per product feature (Section 10.4).
\end{itemize}

We fix the thresholds on the development set before running the test set, and we will report a null result as a finding.

\section{Why It Matters, and What Is Missing}

\subsection{Why It Matters}

Undertrial prisoners made up 75.8 percent of India's prison population at the end of 2022 [\href{https://www.ncrb.gov.in/uploads/nationalcrimerecordsbureau/custom/psiyearwise2022/1701613297PSI2022ason01122023.pdf}{3}]. Many depend on legal-aid lawyers with heavy caseloads, so a weak or late bail application can mean months more in custody. Legal-aid offices also need to find, among hundreds of files, the few where a statutory right (for example default bail) is about to open or lapse.

The IPC-to-BNS change adds a new source of mistakes that is easy to miss. A lawyer, or a tool, may cite the wrong section number, or rely on a judgment about wording that no longer exists.

\textbf{How the fine-tuning data is made, with no humans:}

\begin{enumerate}
\item The teacher reads the facts section of about 4,000 train bail orders and outputs a dossier, quoting a source sentence for each field.
\item An entailment check (\st{span\_\allowbreak{}check}) tests whether each quote supports its value.
\item We keep only the orders where every field passes, an estimated 2,000 pairs.
\item For 30 percent of the kept pairs, the input is replaced by OCR output of a degraded rendering of the same text (Augraphy [\href{https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.14558}{25}] with Surya). The target dossier is unchanged, so the extractor learns to read OCR noise.
\item QLoRA trains the 4B (rank 16, two epochs, one RTX 2080 Ti).
\end{enumerate}

The teacher only ever sees public court orders. \textbf{The fine-tuning claim we test:} the tuned 4B comes close to the prompted 32B on dossier accuracy, on clean and noisy input, at a fraction of the memory and latency.

\textbf{Case record.} Every agent action is appended to an event log in SQLite: who acted, which tool, inputs, outputs, sources and time. The dossier, timeline, brief and sparring record are views computed from it. One mechanism gives four uses: an audit trail, resuming after an Ada session ends, dependency tracking for incremental redrafting, and the live agent trace in the interface. The agents run as a LangGraph graph with a SQLite checkpointer, and the two approval gates are interrupts that wait for counsel (fallback: a plain Python state machine with the same log). Plans and tool calls use JSON-schema-constrained decoding, so every call parses.

\subsection{Munshi: Ingesting Any Kind of PDF}

Munshi handles ten input kinds. Each page climbs a routing ladder only as far as it needs to, so the costly vision model sees only the pages that need it.

{\small
\begin{xltabular}{\linewidth}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash\hsize=0.57\hsize}X>{\raggedright\arraybackslash\hsize=1.10\hsize}X>{\raggedright\arraybackslash\hsize=1.33\hsize}X@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Kind} & \textbf{Example} & \textbf{Route} \\
\midrule
\endhead
K1 Born-digital & High Court order from the court website & Text layer; layout parser (Docling) for tables and columns \\
K2 Scanned & Sessions Court order scanned at a legal-aid office & Clean-up (deskew, denoise), then OCR \\
K3 Degraded scan & Faint photocopy, stamps over text & Stronger clean-up and re-OCR, then the vision model \\
K4 Phone photo & Photo of an FIR copy & Dewarp and crop, then as K2/K3 \\
K5 Hindi or mixed script & Hindi FIR & Hindi OCR, then translation; both texts kept \\
K6 Legacy Hindi fonts & Kruti Dev-style PDF whose text layer is gibberish & Ignore the text layer; OCR the page image \\
K7 Handwriting & Handwritten FIR entries & Vision model; if unreadable, ask counsel \\
K8 Bundle & One PDF with the FIR, two remand orders and a bail order & Split into documents, then route each page \\
K9 Locked or broken file & Password-protected or corrupt PDF & Ask counsel for a copy; never guess \\
K10 Not a PDF & DOCX, images, typed chat, voice & Read directly; voice goes to speech recognition \\
\bottomrule
\end{xltabular}}

\textbf{Ladder:} (1) text layer (PyMuPDF), (2) layout parser (Docling), (3) clean-up and OCR (Surya, Tesseract as fallback), (4) vision model (Qwen3-VL, asked only to transcribe), (5) ask counsel. Quality signals per page (text coverage, OCR confidence, dictionary-word share, script consistency, legacy-font patterns) decide when to climb; thresholds are tuned on dev, and each page's route is shown as a badge in the interface.

\textbf{Agent loop.} Munshi:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Surveys} every page and runs the cheapest acceptable route.
\item \textbf{Categorises:} splits bundles into documents, labels each document (FIR, arrest memo, remand order, bail order, custody certificate and so on), and tags paragraphs by the kind of information they hold (dated event, party, section, earlier outcome, custody, personal circumstance).
\item \textbf{Files} each item into the case shelf with its source (document, page, box, route, language, confidence). Names are pseudonymised first.
\item \textbf{Checks gaps} against a bail-file checklist (FIR, arrest date, first remand, earlier bail orders, charge-sheet status, custody certificate, co-accused orders). For each gap it re-reads a likely page with a stronger route, searches the remaining pages, or asks counsel.
\item \textbf{Checks conflicts:} if two documents disagree, both values are kept and passed to Peshkar as \st{AMBIGUOUS}.
\end{enumerate}

Steps 4 and 5 make this an agent rather than a fixed pipeline; variant P5 removes them to measure the effect. Munshi and Peshkar share a limit of 3 questions per round. For Hindi, every quote keeps the Hindi original and its English translation. Counsel can also dictate facts in Hindi or English; Munshi reads back the dates, sections and numbers it heard for confirmation, and dictated facts are marked \st{USER\_\allowbreak{}STATED}.

\subsection{The Shelves: Ten Stores, Four Index Kinds}

A bail brief needs exact statute text, a graph from old sections to new ones, court reasoning, citation history, what each side usually argues, and the client's own record. Each has a different shape and a different right way to search it, so we use ten stores, not one index.

\toprule
\textbf{Shelf} & \textbf{Store} & \textbf{Contents} & \textbf{Index} \\
\midrule
\endhead
Law & \st{law.statutes} & One row per section of the IPC, BNS, CrPC, BNSS, plus NDPS s.37, UAPA s.43D(5), PMLA s.45 (about 1,000 rows) & Exact lookup + BM25 \\
Law & \st{law.concordance} & IPC to BNS and CrPC to BNSS edges: same, split, merged, reworded, new (about 600 edges) & Graph \\
Case law & \st{caselaw.reasoning} & The court's reasoning paragraphs (est. 100 to 300K) & Hybrid BM25 + BGE-M3, metadata filter \\
Case law & \st{caselaw.citations} & Who cites whom, with the treatment (followed, distinguished, overruled) & Graph + authority score \\
Case law & \st{caselaw.similar} & One vector per order's facts, plus a fact signature (est. 50 to 100K orders) & Whole-order vectors + structured match \\
Advocacy & \st{advocacy.arguments} & Defense-submission paragraphs, tagged by factor (est. 100K); never cited as authority & Hybrid + filters \\
Advocacy & \st{advocacy.objections} & Prosecution-submission paragraphs, tagged by factor (est. 80K) & Hybrid + filters \\
Case (private) & \st{case.docs} & The client's documents in structure-aware chunks with page and box & Hybrid \\
Case (private) & \st{case.timeline} & Dated events with sources & Exact lookup \\
Case (private) & \st{case.facts} & The \st{CaseDossier}, each field with status and source & Exact lookup \\
\bottomrule
\end{xltabular}}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Chunking and metadata.} Each order is split by cue phrases into facts, defense submissions, prosecution submissions, reasoning and operative order, then at numbered paragraphs (up to 400 tokens). Every paragraph is tagged automatically (mostly by rules) with its court, date, outcome, part, bail factors, \st{section\_\allowbreak{}family} and citation links. Authorities are cited from \st{reasoning} paragraphs only.
\item \textbf{Citation graph and good-law check.} Regexes find Indian citations and resolve them against Open India Law (we report the resolution rate). Cue words in the citing sentence, confirmed by Llama-3.1-8B (only agreed labels), give the treatment. An authority score ranks by court level, bench size, citations and recency. A precedent treated as overruled by a later SC or larger bench cannot be cited without a warning, and for a High Court paragraph the graph is followed to the SC judgment behind it.
\item \textbf{Recodification bridge.} A BNS case asking about BNS 318(4) also retrieves precedents decided under IPC 420. Each retrieved precedent gets a \textbf{transfer flag}: \st{SAME\_\allowbreak{}TEXT} (the provision was carried over unchanged), \st{CHANGED} (split, merged or reworded, so the brief must qualify its use) or \st{NEW} (no IPC equivalent).
\item \textbf{Storage.} SQLite (statutes, timelines, graph edges, event log) and LanceDB (vectors and full-text search), all files on disk, so the same stores run on the laptop and on Ada.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Sutradhar: The Orchestrator}

Each of the 12 bail factors (for example custody period, parity with co-accused, charge sheet filed) has an \textbf{evidence contract}: a short list of the evidence a ground needs and which stores may hold it. For parity: the co-accused's order and a role comparison (required, from the case shelf), two authorities (from \st{caselaw.reasoning} and \st{caselaw.similar}), a good-law check, one likely objection. The contract fixes \emph{what} is needed and \emph{where it may be}. Sutradhar decides the rest:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Picks factors:} reads the approved dossier and rights report, and marks each factor favourable, adverse (to be answered) or not applicable, with a reason.
\item \textbf{For each factor, in parallel:} writes a \st{RetrievalPlan}, runs the calls, marks each slot \st{FILLED}, \st{WEAK} or \st{MISSING} using rule checks, and re-plans weak slots (reword the query, relax filters in a fixed order, follow the citation graph to the SC authority, try another allowed store). It stops when every required slot is filled or the budget is spent (default 150K tokens per case and 8 store calls per factor). Missing slots become open issues in the brief.
\item \textbf{Delegates:} passes evidence to Vakeel, the draft to Abhiyojak, objections back to Vakeel, and decides whether a failed claim needs new retrieval or a rewording.
\item \textbf{After a change,} re-runs only the affected factors (7.8).
\end{enumerate}

Sutradhar also routes counsel's chat questions: custody days go to the timeline and \st{date\_\allowbreak{}calc}, section equivalents to the concordance, eligibility to the Rights Radar, case-law questions to the case-law shelf, and ``what did the Sessions Court say'' to \st{case.docs}. Answers are short and cited.

\textbf{Why this is more than ``query everything''.} B4 queries every store and fuses the results. A6 runs the contracts in a fixed order with fixed queries. A7 lets the model plan freely with no contracts. Comparing these with the full system shows whether gains come from the stores, from the contracts, or from the agent's planning and stopping.

Each field holds a value, a source (page, box, route, language, quote) and one status:

\subsection{Vakeel, Abhiyojak and Incremental Redrafting}

\textbf{Vakeel} (tools: \st{get\_\allowbreak{}paragraph}, \st{get\_\allowbreak{}statute}, \st{check\_\allowbreak{}claim}, \st{transfer\_\allowbreak{}check}, \st{good\_\allowbreak{}law\_\allowbreak{}check}, \st{assemble\_\allowbreak{}application}, and \st{export}, which is gated) drafts one ground per chosen factor, where every sentence cites dossier facts and authority paragraphs. It then checks every claim: facts against the dossier quote and authorities against the cited paragraph (entailment), plus the transfer flag, the good-law flag, and that no citation was invented. A failed claim is repaired, by re-retrieving through Sutradhar or rewording, for at most two rounds; otherwise it is marked \st{NEEDS\_\allowbreak{}REVIEW}. \textbf{Nothing is dropped silently.} The model writes only document IDs, and citation text is rendered from corpus metadata, so it cannot invent a case name. The \st{BailBrief} records each ground's factor, argument, facts, authorities with flags, check status (\st{SUPPORTED}, \st{REPAIRED}, \st{NEEDS\_\allowbreak{}REVIEW}) and its dependencies.

\textbf{Court-ready output.} A python-docx template is filled from the brief: cause title, application under section 483 BNSS, synopsis, List of Dates and Events, brief facts, earlier bail applications, grounds, statutory position, prayer, verification placeholders and an annexure index. The model writes only the synopsis, brief facts and prayer, and every date comes from the timeline with its source. A one-page \textbf{hearing-prep card} lists the concerns the bench is likely to raise (from comparable orders), three points to lead with, conditions courts often impose in similar grants, and open issues. It makes no outcome prediction and shows no grant rates.

\textbf{Abhiyojak} sees only the drafted grounds, the dossier and its own stores (\st{advocacy.objections}, refusal reasoning, statutes), never Vakeel's plan, so it cannot simply agree. In round 1 it raises up to two objections per ground plus general ones (gravity, flight risk, witness tampering, antecedents, statutory bars); each must cite an objection-bank paragraph, a refusal paragraph, a statute or a dossier fact, or it is discarded. Vakeel replies under the same checks. In round 2 Abhiyojak marks each reply \st{ANSWERED}, \st{PARTLY} or \st{UNANSWERED}, and unanswered objections go to counsel. This is scored against the objections the prosecution actually raised (G4), whereas courtroom simulations are scored on the verdict.

\textbf{Incremental redrafting.} Each claim stores what it depends on (dossier fields with value hashes, timeline events, rights entries, authority paragraphs). When counsel approves a change set, claims with a changed dependency become \st{STALE}, and so do claims that cite them. Sutradhar re-checks which factors apply and re-plans only the reopened ones; Vakeel redrafts only the stale claims, and Abhiyojak re-spars only on changed grounds. Unchanged claims stay word for word. The idea comes from classical truth-maintenance systems \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/0004-3702(79}{[26}90008-0)], applied here to an LLM agent's draft.

\subsection{Pairokar: The Batch Desk}

A legal-aid office uploads many files. In stage 1, Munshi, Peshkar and the Rights Radar run on every file (cheap) and each case gets a \st{TriageCard} with its urgency, flags and missing documents. Urgency follows a fixed, explainable rule: legal deadlines first (a default-bail window open or opening soon, the s.479 threshold crossed), then days past a threshold, then custody length. Pairokar decides, per case, whether to queue a full brief, flag it to counsel, or ask for documents first, and re-prioritises as results come in. In stage 2, full runs go in urgency order, with a retrieval cache shared across cases and vLLM prefix caching. The output is a cause-list board with each case's next step. There is no bulk export: every case needs its own approval.

\subsection{Features, Frontend and User Flows}

The product is a \textbf{Next.js} web app (TypeScript, Tailwind, shadcn/ui) over a \textbf{FastAPI} backend that streams agent events with Server-Sent Events; the 14B agents on Ada are reached through an SSH tunnel for the demo. It has an animated landing page (a citizen's journey in six scenes) and a small live animation for each agent, so counsel can see who is working. The case workspace has a stage rail with the two gates, a case-file pane with a PDF viewer that highlights sources, a chat, and tabs for the dossier, dates, rights, brief, sparring and hearing prep. Other pages are the cause list, the batch desk and a precedent explorer. The look is a ``manuscript'' theme, with all artwork our own and no use of the State Emblem or court logos.

\section{Novelty}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Agentic ingestion for Indian court files.} Page-level routing with quality gates, bundle splitting, Hindi handling, and gap-driven re-reading, with every item filed into typed stores with its source. Tested on a constructed messy-PDF benchmark whose answers are known.
\item \textbf{Contract-driven orchestration over different kinds of legal store.} Evidence contracts per factor; the orchestrator plans which of ten stores to query, checks sufficiency, re-plans and stops within a budget. B4 separates this from simply having more stores.
\item \textbf{Adversarial grounding scored against the real prosecution.} A sparring agent measured against the objections actually raised in the real order, with a 2$\times$2 ablation that separates the effect of the opponent from that of the verifier.
\item \textbf{Truth maintenance for long-lived agent drafts.} Dependency-tracked incremental redrafting when the case moves on.
\item \textbf{Case law that crosses the IPC to BNS change,} with changed provisions flagged.
\item \textbf{Legal rules as tools, and triage at scale.} A deterministic Rights Radar, and a batch desk that schedules agent work by legal urgency on shared GPUs.
\item \textbf{A dossier-first design that never drops a claim silently,} and a local, noise-robust fine-tuned extractor.
\item \textbf{A fully automatic benchmark suite.} Seven kinds of court-grounded gold label (G1 to G7) and nine test sets (T1 to T9), with no manual annotation, so anyone can rebuild it.
\end{itemize}

Approvals & Export works only after Gate 2, enforced in code, not in a prompt. Batch mode never exports in bulk: each case needs its own approval. Measured on T7. \\
Privacy & Text is pseudonymised after OCR and before it leaves the laptop. Page images, audio and the vision model's runs on client files stay on the laptop; Ada sees only pseudonymised text and public test data. Logs keep only pseudonymised text. Scope & No outcome predictions or grant rates, and no advice to accused persons or the public (the landing page points them to free legal aid, NALSA 15100). Every output is labelled an AI draft. \\

Sensitive matters & Sexual-offence, POCSO and juvenile matters are declined at intake and left out of the evaluation, since the law protects the identity of victims and children (BNS section 72, POCSO section 23, JJ Act section 74). \\
Data and symbols & Only public court orders and constructed files; an external teacher API, if used, sees only public training orders. No State Emblem, national flag or court logos. \\
Bias & Results are compared across offence types, courts, bail outcomes and input kinds (Hindi vs English, scanned vs digital). \\

\section{Compute Plan}

\subsection{Hardware and Rules}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Ada cluster:} three team accounts, each with one node of four RTX 2080 Ti GPUs (11 GB each). Version 5 needs \textbf{all three}.
\item \textbf{Laptop:} an RTX 4060 (8 GB) for development, the demo, the local agents (Munshi, Peshkar) and long, light jobs.
\item \textbf{API budget:} none, apart from a free tier for the teacher, and only if the week-1 pilot picks it.
\item \textbf{Training:} batch jobs (up to four days) are used only for QLoRA, never to host an inference engine. Each run is at most 1.5 days on one GPU.
\item \textbf{Inference:} all model inference runs in interactive sessions that end after six hours. vLLM runs inside the experiment script, so there is no long-running server. Work is cut into shards of about five hours, and each case's output is saved as soon as it finishes, so a session that ends early resumes where it stopped.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Ada Estimate}

Node-hours on one four-GPU node; throughput is assumed and measured in week 1 (about 2M tokens per node-hour for the 14B, 0.8M for the 32B, 800 vision pages per node-hour).

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{GPU-hours:} about 1,120 in interactive sessions plus about 100 for three QLoRA batch runs, so \textbf{about 1,220 RTX 2080 Ti GPU-hours} (about 980 with the API).
\item \textbf{Sessions:} at about 5 useful hours per session, \textbf{about 56 sessions} (about 44 with the API); with a 30 percent buffer, \textbf{about 73} (about 57 with the API). This needs all three accounts at about 4 sessions per account per week over weeks 2 to 7.
\item \textbf{Peak:} weeks 6 and 7, when every run that needs the frozen system comes to about 160 node-h. B0 to B3 and the F3 baselines do not depend on our system, so they run in week 5 to flatten the peak.
\item \textbf{Laptop:} about 90 hours in total (embeddings for about 560K vectors, 20 h; evaluation labels, 30 h; MiniCheck, 10 h; T2 construction and cheap pipelines, 15 h; other, 15 h), overnight in weeks 2 to 7.
\item \textbf{Teacher pilot (week 1):} Qwen3-32B on Ada against a Mistral API model on the same 50 train orders; we pick whichever gives more verified pairs per day, if the API's terms allow it. The teacher and the evaluation labeler must not be the same model family.
\end{itemize}

\section{Risks and Fallback Plans}

{\small
\begin{xltabular}{\linewidth}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash\hsize=0.70\hsize}X>{\raggedright\arraybackslash\hsize=1.30\hsize}X@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Risk} & \textbf{Fallback} \\
\midrule
\endhead
Too few BNS-era bail orders (Open India Law runs to 2025) & Report H1c as a pilot on the cases available \\
Many orders do not split cleanly, or the two labelers often disagree & Use a non-Qwen model to split the failures; use only agreed labels, report coverage, and merge factors that get confused \\
The fine-tuned 4B extractor is weak & Use the prompted 14B on pseudonymised text and report the negative result \\
\textbf{Compute:} about 56 sessions (about 73 with buffer) is roughly twice the earlier plan & Use all three accounts from week 2; use the API teacher if the pilot allows (about 12 fewer sessions); ask hpc.admin about offline batch inference. If still short: main runs from 3 to 2, ablations from 100 to 60 cases, F2 to 40 cases. Cuts shrink samples, never drop a feature's evaluation \\
\textbf{Scope:} 8 features, 6 agents and a rich frontend in 8 weeks & Four parallel workstreams; feature freeze at the end of week 5; static frames first and a time-boxed landing page \\
The vision model is slow or weak on the 2080 Ti & Use Qwen3-VL-4B, or OCR only with ``unreadable, ask counsel''; report \\
Hindi data sources are unavailable & Use HLDC only, or NLLB-translated companion documents \\
Low citation resolution rate & Report coverage; show the good-law badge only for resolved citations \\
The orchestrator loops or overspends & Hard budgets per factor and per case; fall back to the fixed router (A6); report \\
Unsettled law in the Rights Radar & Return \st{CHECK}; show rule text and source; keep a ``last verified'' date per rule \\
Slow GPUs or long queues; the teacher API's terms do not suit us & Saved progress means a lost session costs at most one shard; use Qwen3-32B on Ada \\
\bottomrule
\end{xltabular}}

\section{Deliverables and Scope}

\subsection{Deliverables}

\begin{itemize}
\item A working Next.js prototype with all six agents, chat, voice, the cause list, the batch desk and both approval gates.
\item The automatically built benchmark suite and the scripts that make it.
\item Code, job scripts and full agent traces.
\item A workshop-style report and a demo video.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Later, Not In This Version}

\begin{itemize}
\item bail applications drafted in Hindi (Hindi intake is included);
\item court-specific filing formats for each High Court;
\item drafting default-bail applications (the system only flags the window);
\item anticipatory bail and district courts;
\item a learned router distilled from Sutradhar's plans;
\item live eCourts case-status lookup;
\item counterfactual-record tests and a verification-cost study.
\end{itemize}
